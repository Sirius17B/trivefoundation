# INTRODUCTION TO TECH
## A First Guide to Technology for Young People

---

## How to use this book

Welcome! You do not need to know anything about technology to read this book. Everything starts from the very beginning, and every new word is explained the first time it appears.

Technology can sound big and scary. It is not. It is mostly a set of clever ideas that people worked out step by step, and you can understand those ideas too. You already use technology every day: your phone, the light switch, the POS machine at the shop, the radio. This book helps you see what is really going on inside the things you already use.

Each chapter has the same parts, so you always know where you are:

1. **In this chapter.** A short story to get you thinking, then a list of what you will be able to do by the end.
2. **The ideas.** Short sections, explained in everyday words, with examples from life in Nigeria.
3. **Words to know.** A mini-dictionary of the new words in that chapter.
4. **Try it.** An activity you can do with a pen, paper and a friend. You do not need a computer.
5. **Check yourself.** Two questions with full explanations, so you can see if the ideas have clicked.

There are ten chapters:

1. Computers, Phones and the Internet
2. Artificial Intelligence
3. Machine Learning
4. Chatbots and Large Language Models
5. Robotics
6. Staying Safe Online
7. Programming and Apps
8. Making Websites
9. Data
10. Technology and Everyone

A few tips before you start. Read one chapter at a time, and take your time. If a word is new, that is completely normal; check the Words to know list. When you reach a Check yourself question, try to answer it before you read the explanation. Getting one wrong and then understanding why is one of the best ways to learn. And if something is confusing, ask a teacher, a friend or someone at home. Asking questions is exactly what good tech people do.

# Chapter 1: Computers, Phones and the Internet

## In this chapter

Chidera lives in Awka. Before school, she checks the time on her phone, sends a voice note on WhatsApp to her cousin in Lagos, and plays a song while she packs her bag. Her phone did all of that in a few minutes. So is her phone a computer? By the end of this chapter you will know the answer, and you will see why.

By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

- Explain what a computer is, and name its four jobs: taking in, thinking, remembering and showing.
- Tell hardware and software apart, and give examples of each.
- Explain what the internet is, and tell mobile data from Wi-Fi.
- Explain what "the cloud" really means.

## 1.1 What is a computer?

A **computer** is a machine that follows instructions. That is the whole idea. It does exactly what it is told, very fast, and it never gets tired. But it does not understand anything the way you do. It only follows the instructions it is given.

Every computer does four jobs:

- **Input.** Taking information in. Typing on a keyboard, touching a screen and speaking into a microphone are all input.
- **Processing.** Working with that information. This is the "thinking" part, and it is done by a chip called the **processor**.
- **Storage.** Remembering things so they are still there tomorrow. Your photos, songs and apps are stored in your phone's memory.
- **Output.** Showing you the result. The screen, the speaker and a printer are all output.

When Chidera taps the camera button on her phone, the tap is the input. The phone works out the picture (processing), saves it (storage) and shows it on the screen (output).

Your phone is a computer. So is a laptop. So are the POS machine at the shop, the ATM at the bank, and the small control panel inside many generators and cars. Computers come in many shapes and sizes, and most of them are much smaller than you would guess.

## 1.2 Hardware and software

A computer has two sides: hardware and software.

**Hardware** means the parts you can touch: the screen, the battery, the charger, the buttons and the chips inside. **Software** means the instructions that tell the hardware what to do. You cannot touch software. Apps, games and the system that runs your phone are all software.

Think of a school. The buildings, desks and chairs are like hardware. The timetable and the lessons are like software, because they tell everyone what to do and when. A school with no timetable is just empty rooms. In the same way, a computer with no software is just a box that does nothing.

An **app** is a piece of software built for one job: WhatsApp for messages, a banking app for money, a game for fun. Behind all your apps sits the **operating system**, the main software that keeps everything running and lets the apps share the phone. On most phones in Nigeria it is Android, and on iPhones it is iOS.

## 1.3 What is the internet?

The **internet** is millions of computers and phones all over the world joined together so they can share information. A group of connected computers is called a **network**. The internet is often described as a "network of networks".

When you open a website, your phone sends a request to a faraway computer called a **server**. A server is simply a computer whose job is to give things to other computers. It sends the page back to your phone, and you see it on your screen. All of this happens in a moment.

How does the information travel? Some of it goes through cables. Many of the cables that carry the internet run along the floor of the ocean between countries. Some of it travels as radio signals through the air, which is how your mobile network works.

There are two common ways your phone connects:

- **Mobile data** comes through your SIM card from your phone network, such as MTN, Airtel or Glo. You buy a bundle, and when the bundle is finished, you are cut off until you buy more.
- **Wi-Fi** is a wireless link to a box called a router, in a home, school or shop. The router is connected to the internet. Using Wi-Fi does not use up your data bundle.

You have probably heard of **the cloud**. It sounds like something floating in the sky, but it is not. The cloud simply means files kept on servers somewhere else, instead of on your own phone. If your photos or WhatsApp chats are backed up online, they are sitting on computers in a big building full of servers.

## 1.4 Words to know

- **computer**: a machine that follows instructions. It takes in information, works on it, stores it and shows a result.
- **processor**: the chip that does the "thinking" inside a computer.
- **hardware**: the parts of a computer that you can touch.
- **software**: the instructions that tell the hardware what to do.
- **app**: software made to do one job, such as sending messages.
- **operating system**: the main software that runs the device and its apps, for example Android.
- **internet**: computers and phones around the world, connected so they can share information.
- **network**: a group of computers connected together.
- **server**: a computer that gives information to other computers.
- **Wi-Fi**: a wireless connection to a router.
- **mobile data**: internet that comes through your SIM card and your data bundle.
- **the cloud**: files stored on servers somewhere else, not on your own device.

## 1.5 Try it

You do not need a device for this one. Draw four boxes on a sheet of paper and label them Input, Processing, Storage and Output. Pick something you do on a phone, such as sending a voice note or taking a photo, and write down what happens in each box. Then do the same for a POS machine at a shop or an ATM. What is the input? What is the output? You will find that every computer follows the same four steps.

## 1.6 Check yourself

**Example 1**

*Tunde lives in Lagos. He says, "My phone cannot be a computer, because a computer is a big machine that sits on a desk." Which reply is best?*

A) You are right, because a machine must have a keyboard and a large screen before it can count as a real computer
B) Not quite, because a computer is any machine that takes in information, works on it, stores it and shows a result, and a phone does all four
C) You are right, because phones are only made for calls and messages, while computers are made for all the other jobs
D) Not quite, because a phone only becomes a computer once it is connected to the internet, and not before that moment

*Walk through it:* Choice A and choice C both describe what computers often look like, but looks are not what makes something a computer. Choice D mixes up the computer with the internet: a phone still follows instructions and does its four jobs when it is offline, for example when you take a photo. A computer is defined by what it does, not by its size, and your phone takes in information, processes it, stores it and shows results. So the answer is **B**.

**Example 2**

*Amina watches a video on her phone using the school Wi-Fi. Later at home there is no Wi-Fi, so she watches the same video using her mobile data bundle. What is different between the two times?*

A) At school the video comes from the cloud, but at home it plays from the memory inside her phone instead
B) Only the school Wi-Fi uses a server to send the video, because mobile data reaches videos without needing one
C) With Wi-Fi the video does not travel over the internet at all, but with mobile data it does travel over it
D) The video travels over the internet both times, but at home it is counted against her data bundle and at school it is not

*Walk through it:* Whether she uses Wi-Fi or mobile data, the video is not stored on her phone. In both cases it is asked for from a server and sent across the internet. That rules out choice A, choice B and choice C, which all pretend the two ways of connecting are very different underneath. The real difference is who pays: mobile data comes out of her bundle, while the school Wi-Fi does not. So the answer is **D**.

# Chapter 2: Artificial Intelligence

## In this chapter

Zainab lives in Abuja. She says to her phone, "Call Mummy," and it does. Her cousin laughs and says, "Wow, your phone is so intelligent!" But is it really? In this chapter you will find out what people mean when they say AI, where you already meet it, and what it cannot do.

By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

- Explain what artificial intelligence (AI) is in your own words.
- Give three examples of AI that you may already use.
- Explain why today's AI is "narrow", meaning it is good at one job only.
- Say some things that AI cannot do.

## 2.1 What is AI?

The word **artificial** means made by people. **Intelligence** means being able to learn, understand and solve problems. So **artificial intelligence**, or **AI**, is when people build computer programs that can do jobs which normally need human intelligence.

Those jobs include understanding speech, recognising faces and pictures, translating between languages, spotting patterns and making suggestions.

In Chapter 1 you learned that a computer follows instructions. An ordinary program follows steps that a person wrote down one by one. AI is different. In most AI, the computer works out a lot of its own rules by studying many examples. You will see how in the next chapter.

## 2.2 AI you already meet

You probably use AI every day without noticing it:

- **Voice assistants.** When Zainab says "Call Mummy", the phone turns her voice into words and works out what she wants.
- **Maps.** A maps app suggests the quickest route and warns you about traffic, using information from lots of phones.
- **Recommendations.** When a video app suggests the next video you might like, a program has guessed that from what you and other people watched.
- **Translation.** Apps such as Google Translate can help translate between many languages, including Hausa, Igbo and Yoruba.
- **Face unlock.** Your phone can learn to recognise your face.
- **Typing help.** When your keyboard guesses the next word or fixes a spelling mistake, a program is predicting what you meant.
- **Bank alerts.** Many banks use AI-type systems to notice unusual spending and send a warning message.

## 2.3 Narrow AI: very good at one thing

Every AI that exists today is **narrow AI**. That means it is built for one kind of job, and it can do only that job. The AI that suggests videos cannot drive a car. The AI that unlocks your phone with your face cannot translate Igbo. A chess program can beat the best human players in the world, but it cannot tell you whether it will rain tomorrow.

There is another idea called **general AI**, a machine that could learn to do anything a human can do. It does not exist. You will see it in films, but nobody has built it. So when someone says "the computer is so clever", remember that it is clever at one thing, and it only looks clever because that one thing is so hard.

## 2.4 What AI cannot do

AI is useful, but there are some common wrong ideas about it:

- **AI does not have feelings.** When a chatbot says "I'm happy to help", it is following patterns in language. It is not really happy.
- **AI is not magic.** It was built by people, and people chose the examples it learned from.
- **AI makes mistakes.** A voice assistant might mishear "Call Mummy" as "Call Money". It is funny when it happens, but it is a reminder that AI is guessing.
- **AI is not always right just because a computer said it.** A person should always be ready to check.

## 2.5 Words to know

- **artificial**: made by people, not natural.
- **artificial intelligence (AI)**: computer programs that do jobs which normally need human intelligence.
- **narrow AI**: AI that is good at one job only. All AI today is narrow AI.
- **general AI**: a machine that could do anything a human can. It does not exist yet.
- **voice assistant**: an AI that listens to what you say and does what you ask.
- **recommendation**: a suggestion made by a program, based on what you and others have done.
- **pattern**: something that repeats in a way that a program can spot.

## 2.6 Try it

Spot the AI. For one day, write down five things you use on a phone, a TV or a computer that might use AI. For each one, write down the one job it is doing. Then ask yourself: could it do a completely different job as well? If the answer is no, you have just found an example of narrow AI.

## 2.7 Check yourself

**Example 1**

*Emeka's uncle says, "The app that recommends videos on Emeka's phone is so smart that it must also be able to drive a car." What is the best answer?*

A) That is true, because AI that is clever at one job is automatically clever at every other job as well
B) That is true, but only after the app has been connected to the internet and given enough data
C) That is not true, because today's AI is built for one kind of job, and being good at one job does not make it good at others
D) That is not true, because recommending videos is not really AI at all, and real AI only exists inside robots and cars

*Walk through it:* Choice A and choice B both imagine that cleverness spreads from one job to another, but it does not. All AI today is narrow, built for one job. Choice D goes the other way and says video recommendations are not AI, which is wrong, because suggesting videos is a very common example of AI. The correct idea is that the app is good at one job and cannot do the others. So the answer is **C**.

**Example 2**

*Ifeoma's phone suggests a reply to a message: "Okay, I'm coming!" She says, "My phone understands me and is happy to help." What is the most accurate thing to say?*

A) The phone found a pattern in many example messages and predicted a likely reply, but it does not understand or feel anything
B) The phone really does understand her messages in the way a close friend would, because it is a type of artificial intelligence
C) The phone is only using fixed replies that a programmer typed in by hand, one at a time, for every possible message
D) The phone is copying the reply straight from the person she is chatting with, without using any rules or patterns at all

*Walk through it:* Choice B is the common mistake of thinking that AI understands and feels, when it is following patterns. Choice C is also wrong, because no programmer could type a fixed reply for every possible message. That is why the phone uses patterns it learned from examples. Choice D is not how a suggested reply works either. The phone predicts a likely reply from patterns, which is what choice A says. So the answer is **A**.

# Chapter 3: Machine Learning

## In this chapter

Ngozi helps her aunt at a fruit stall in Onitsha. Her little brother is learning to tell a mango from an orange. Nobody gives him a rulebook. Instead, he is shown lots of fruit, and each time someone says "mango" or "orange". After a while he can tell the difference, even for a mango he has never seen before. A computer can learn in a very similar way, and this is called machine learning.

By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

- Explain machine learning as learning from examples instead of following written rules.
- Explain training data, labels and a model in simple words.
- Describe three ways that a machine can learn.
- Explain why limited or unfair examples make a machine make mistakes.

## 3.1 Rules or examples?

An ordinary program follows rules that a programmer writes. Imagine we want a program that blocks spam messages. We could write a rule: "If the message has the word lottery, block it." But scammers soon use other words. We would need thousands of rules, and we would never finish.

**Machine learning** solves this in a different way. Instead of writing the rules, we show the computer lots of examples and let it find the patterns itself. We show it thousands of spam messages and thousands of normal messages. The computer studies them and works out what spam usually looks like. Machine learning is the part of AI that learns from examples, and most AI you meet today is built this way.

## 3.2 How a machine learns

Learning from examples has a few steps, which we can follow with the mango and the orange:

1. **Collect examples.** Gather thousands of pictures of mangoes and oranges. This collection is called **training data**.
2. **Add labels.** For each picture, add the correct answer: "mango" or "orange". The correct answer attached to an example is called a **label**.
3. **Train.** The computer studies all the pictures and labels and looks for patterns, such as shapes, colours and textures. This is called **training**.
4. **Build a model.** What the computer learns is saved as a **model**. A model is a program that can make a guess about something new.
5. **Test.** Show the model fruit it has never seen before and check if it is right. If it makes too many mistakes, go back and improve the examples.

The word **data** simply means information, such as pictures, numbers, words or sounds.

## 3.3 Three ways to learn

Machines can learn in three main ways:

- **From labelled examples.** This is like flash cards with the answers on the back. A computer is shown messages already marked "spam" or "not spam", and learns to tell them apart.
- **By finding groups on its own.** Here there are no answers given. A shopkeeper's computer looks at what 5,000 customers bought and finds that they fall into groups, such as people who buy school items and people who buy food. Nobody told it what the groups should be.
- **By trial and reward.** This is like learning to ride a bicycle, or playing a video game. The computer tries things, gets points for good moves and loses points for bad ones. Over time it learns which moves work best.

## 3.4 When learning goes wrong

A machine only knows what its examples taught it. If Ngozi's brother only ever saw yellow mangoes, he might say that a green mango is not a mango. A computer is exactly the same.

Here is a real kind of problem. A voice system trained mostly on one kind of accent can make many more mistakes with people who speak differently. The computer is not being unkind. It simply never heard enough examples. This is called **bias**: when a model gives worse results for some people or things because of the examples it learned from.

How do we fix it? We use examples from many different people and situations, and we test the model on all of them before we trust it. Good examples matter even more than a huge number of examples.

## 3.5 Words to know

- **machine learning**: a way for computers to learn from examples instead of following rules written by a person.
- **data**: information, such as pictures, numbers, words or sounds.
- **training data**: the examples a computer learns from.
- **label**: the correct answer attached to an example.
- **training**: when a computer studies examples to find patterns.
- **model**: what a computer has learned; a program that makes guesses about new things.
- **testing**: checking a model on new examples to see how often it is right.
- **bias**: when a model works worse for some people or things because of the examples it learned from.

## 3.6 Try it

Train a friend, just like a computer. Think of a secret rule for sorting things, such as "things that are round" or "words that start with the letter B". Show your friend five examples that fit the rule and five that do not, but do not say the rule. Then show a new item and ask where it belongs. If your friend guesses right, you have trained a model! If they get it wrong, was your set of examples too small, or did it only show one kind of thing?

## 3.7 Check yourself

**Example 1**

*A team teaches a computer to recognise jollof rice by showing it 10,000 photos. Every photo shows jollof rice on a white plate. Later, the computer fails to recognise jollof rice served in a pot. What is the best explanation?*

A) The computer needs a much faster processor, because speed is what decides whether it can recognise new things
B) Jollof rice in a pot is a different kind of food from jollof rice on a plate, so the computer was right to reject it
C) The programmer forgot to write a rule for pots, and a separate rule must be written by hand for every kind of dish
D) The examples it learned from only showed one situation, so it never learned what jollof rice looks like in other situations

*Walk through it:* Choice A blames speed, but a faster computer would learn the same wrong lesson from the same photos. Choice C talks about writing rules by hand, which is the old way of programming, not machine learning. Choice B says the computer was right, but jollof rice is the same food in a pot or on a plate. The problem is the training data: every example showed a plate, just like the boy who only saw yellow mangoes. So the answer is **D**.

**Example 2**

*A shopkeeper's computer looks at what 5,000 customers bought and finds three groups of shoppers. Nobody told the computer the right answers, or what the groups should be. Which way of learning is this?*

A) Learning from labelled examples, because every customer was marked with the correct group before the computer saw them
B) Learning by finding groups on its own, because no one gave it the right answers or the group names first
C) Learning by trial and reward, because the computer received points every time it correctly found a customer
D) Not machine learning at all, because machine learning only works on pictures and never on numbers like shopping lists

*Walk through it:* Choice A needs labels, and there were none. Choice C needs points for good moves, and there were none either. Choice D is wrong because machine learning works on many kinds of data, including numbers and shopping lists. When there are no answers given and the computer finds groups by itself, that is the second way of learning from Section 3.3. So the answer is **B**.

# Chapter 4: Chatbots and Large Language Models

## In this chapter

Segun lives in Ibadan. One evening, when the light goes off again, he types to a chatbot: "Write a funny poem about NEPA taking the light." A few seconds later, a poem appears on his screen, and it is actually funny. How did a computer do that? And can he trust everything it tells him?

By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

- Explain what a chatbot and a large language model (LLM) are.
- Explain, in simple words, how a chatbot builds its answer.
- Write a clear prompt that gets a better answer.
- Explain why a chatbot can be confidently wrong, and what to do about it.

## 4.1 What is a chatbot?

A **chatbot** is a program you can have a conversation with, by typing or speaking. Some chatbots are very simple. When you call a company and a voice says "Press 1 for your balance", that is a simple chatbot that can only follow a fixed menu.

Newer chatbots, such as ChatGPT, Gemini and Claude, can answer almost any question and write stories, poems, emails and even computer code. They are powered by something called a **large language model**, or **LLM**. An LLM is a machine learning model (Chapter 3) that was trained on a huge amount of writing, such as books, websites and articles. By studying all that writing, it learns how words usually fit together.

## 4.2 How does it write an answer?

Try finishing this sentence: "Please pass me the ..." You might say "salt" or "water". You guessed because you have heard sentences like it many times before.

An LLM does something like this, but at a huge scale. It has studied billions of sentences. When you ask a question, it picks the most likely next word, then the next, then the next, until it has built a full answer.

This tells us two important things. First, the chatbot does not look up the answer in a book. It builds the answer word by word. Second, it does not "know" things the way you do. It is very good at producing writing that sounds right, which is not the same as being right.

## 4.3 Prompts: how to ask well

The words you type to a chatbot are called a **prompt**. A better prompt usually gets a better answer. Here are some tips:

- **Say exactly what you want.** Not "Tell me about football", but "Explain the offside rule".
- **Say who it is for.** For example, "in simple words for a 13-year-old".
- **Say how long or what shape.** For example, "in five short sentences" or "as a list".
- **Ask follow-up questions.** If the answer is too hard, say "Explain it again in simpler words."

Compare these two prompts. A weak one: "Tell me about solar panels." A better one: "Explain how a solar panel turns sunlight into electricity, in simple words for a 13-year-old, using five short sentences." The second one tells the chatbot what, who and how long.

## 4.4 Chatbots can be wrong

Here is the most important thing in this chapter. A chatbot can write an answer that sounds very sure and is completely wrong. This is called a **hallucination**. It happens because the chatbot is building sentences that sound right, not sentences it has checked.

So, what should you do?

- **Check important facts** in a trusted place, such as your textbook, your teacher or an official website.
- **Do not share private information** such as your full name, address, phone number, passwords or photos of documents.
- **Use it to help you learn, not to do your work for you.** If you copy an answer you do not understand, you learn nothing, and your teacher will probably notice. Ask the chatbot to explain things, and then try to explain them back in your own words.

## 4.5 Words to know

- **chatbot**: a program you can talk to by typing or speaking.
- **large language model (LLM)**: a machine learning model trained on a huge amount of writing, which can produce human-sounding text.
- **prompt**: the words you type to tell a chatbot what you want.
- **hallucination**: when a chatbot gives a confident answer that is wrong or made up.
- **trusted source**: a place you can rely on for facts, such as a textbook, a teacher or an official website.

## 4.6 Try it

Do a prompt makeover. On paper, write a weak prompt, such as "Tell me about rice". Then rewrite it three times, each time adding more detail: who it is for, how long it should be, and what you want it to include. Swap with a friend and see whose prompt is clearest. If you have permission to use a chatbot, a teacher or parent can help you compare the answers to the weak and the better prompts.

## 4.7 Check yourself

**Example 1**

*A chatbot is asked, "Which country won the Olympic football gold medal in Atlanta in 1996?" It answers with great confidence, "Brazil won the gold medal." In fact, Nigeria won. What does this show?*

A) Chatbots look up every answer in an official list of facts, so the official list must have contained an error
B) Chatbots are only unreliable about very old events, and anything from recent years can be trusted fully
C) A chatbot can write a confident answer that is wrong, so important facts should be checked in a trusted source
D) The chatbot lied on purpose, because chatbots can decide to trick people whenever they want to

*Walk through it:* Choice A is wrong because a chatbot builds its answers word by word and does not look in a list. Choice B is wrong because chatbots can be wrong about recent events too. Choice D gives the chatbot a plan to trick people, but it has no such plan: it simply produced words that sounded right. This is a hallucination, which is why you should check important facts. So the answer is **C**.

**Example 2**

*Which prompt is most likely to get a useful answer from a chatbot?*

A) Explain how a solar panel turns sunlight into electricity, in simple words for a 13-year-old, using five short sentences
B) Tell me everything there is to know about solar panels, electricity, batteries and houses, and make the answer as long as you can
C) Solar panels are very good for homes and offices, so please explain them to me properly and do not leave anything out
D) Write a long answer about electricity and panels that is as detailed as possible and covers every possible topic

*Walk through it:* A good prompt says what you want, who it is for and how long it should be. Choice A does all three. Choice B and choice D ask for everything and make the answer long, which gives the chatbot no clear target. Choice C only says the panels are good and asks for a full explanation without saying who it is for or how long. So the answer is **A**.

# Chapter 5: Robotics

## In this chapter

Amina lives in Kano. She watches a video of a robot arm in a factory, picking oranges off a belt and putting them in different boxes. Her little brother asks, "Can it see the oranges? Is it thinking?" In this chapter you will find out how a robot really works, what it is made of, and what it can and cannot do.

By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

- Explain what a robot is, and how it is different from an ordinary machine.
- Describe how a robot senses, thinks and acts.
- Name the main parts of a robot and what each one does.
- Say what robots are good at and what is still hard for them.

## 5.1 What is a robot?

A **robot** is a machine that can sense what is around it, make decisions, and do something physical, usually by following a computer program.

Not every machine is a robot. A ceiling fan only spins when you switch it on. It does not notice anything. A robot fan would notice that the room is hot, decide to switch on, and then spin faster or slower as the room changes.

Robots do not have to look like people. Many robots are just an arm, a set of wheels or a small flying machine called a **drone**.

## 5.2 Sense, think, act

Think about cooking. Chioma is watching a pot of stew on the fire. She sees that it is boiling over (she **senses** it). She decides to turn down the gas (she **thinks**). She turns the knob (she **acts**).

Robots work in the same loop, over and over:

- **Sense.** Robots use parts called **sensors** to find out about the world. A camera sees. A microphone hears. A touch sensor feels pressure. A distance sensor measures how far away something is. A temperature sensor tells how hot it is.
- **Think.** A small computer called the **controller** runs the robot's program. It reads what the sensors say and decides what to do next.
- **Act.** Parts called **actuators** make the robot move or do something. Motors turn wheels and arms. A gripper picks things up. A speaker talks. A light flashes.

## 5.3 The parts of a robot

You can compare a robot to a person:

- The **body** is the frame that holds everything together.
- The **sensors** are the robot's eyes, ears and skin.
- The **controller** is the robot's brain.
- The **actuators** are the robot's muscles.
- The **power supply**, usually a battery, is the robot's food.

Take a farm drone as an example. Its camera (sensor) spots dry patches in a field. Its controller decides which patches need water. Its sprayer (actuator) waters only those patches. It saves water, and it does the work faster than a person could.

## 5.4 What robots are good at, and what is hard

Robots are very good at:

- **Repeating the same job** many thousands of times without getting tired or bored.
- **Being precise**, for example placing tiny parts in exactly the right spot.
- **Going where it is too dangerous for people**, such as very hot places.
- **Lifting heavy things.**

But some things that are easy for you are very hard for a robot. Picking up an egg without crushing it, folding clothes, understanding a joke or coping with a messy room full of new objects are all difficult. A robot only does what its program and its sensors allow. It cannot decide by itself to learn a completely new job.

People are still needed to design robots, build them, programme them, repair them and decide how they should be used.

## 5.5 Words to know

- **robot**: a machine that can sense its surroundings, make decisions and do physical actions.
- **sensor**: a part that collects information, such as a camera or a microphone.
- **controller**: the small computer that acts as the robot's brain.
- **actuator**: a part that makes a robot move or act, such as a motor.
- **drone**: a small flying robot.
- **program**: the list of instructions a robot follows.
- **autonomous**: able to work on its own, without someone controlling every move.
- **remote-controlled**: controlled by a person from a distance.

## 5.6 Try it

Be the robot. Work with a friend. One of you is the programmer and the other is the robot. The programmer writes exact instructions on paper to guide the robot from the classroom door to a book on a desk, for example "take three steps forward, turn right, take two steps". The robot must do only what is written, exactly. Where did it go wrong? What was missing? Rewrite the instructions until the robot succeeds. You have just learned why programming robots needs careful, clear steps.

A) The spray nozzle, because it is the part that actually touches the plants in the field
B) The camera, because it is the part that takes in information about the field
C) The computer program, because it is the part that decides which patches get water
D) The battery, because the drone cannot do anything at all without its power supply

*Walk through it:* The sense, think, act loop has three steps. The spray nozzle in choice A is an actuator, so it belongs to the "act" step. The computer program in choice C does the deciding, so it belongs to the "think" step. The battery in choice D gives the drone power, but it does not take in information. The camera collects information about the field, so it is the sensor. So the answer is **B**.

# Chapter 6: Staying Safe Online

## In this chapter

Kemi lives in Ibadan. One afternoon her mother's phone buzzes with an SMS: "Your bank account will be blocked today. Click this link and send your OTP to keep it open." Her mother panics. Kemi stops her and says, "Mummy, wait. Let us check first." Why was Kemi so careful? In this chapter you will learn how people try to trick us online, and how to stay safe.

By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

- Explain what cybersecurity is and why it matters.
- Spot the warning signs of a scam message, also called phishing.
- Explain why you must never share your PIN or OTP, and how to make a strong password.
- Name simple habits that keep your phone and accounts safe.

## 6.1 What is cybersecurity?

The word **cyber** means to do with computers and the internet. **Cybersecurity** means protecting people, devices and information from being stolen, damaged or misused.

It is like locking your door at night, but for your phone and your accounts. Most of us would never leave our house open, but many people leave their online life open without knowing.

Remember from Chapter 1 that a network connects many devices. That is very useful, but it also means one unsafe device can put others at risk. This is why safety habits matter for everyone.

## 6.2 Tricks to watch for

The most common online trick is **phishing**. A phishing message pretends to come from someone you trust, such as a bank, a teacher, a company or a relative, and it tries to make you give away secrets or click a bad link. Some scams also promise a prize or a job.

Here are the warning signs:

- **Hurry.** "Do it today or your account will be blocked!" Scammers want you to act before you think.
- **Asking for secrets.** A real bank will never ask for your PIN, your OTP or your password. Neither will a real teacher.
- **Strange links.** A link that does not look like the real website, or that comes from a number or address you do not know.
- **Too good to be true.** "You have won a million naira!" If you did not enter anything, you did not win anything.
- **Messy writing.** Many scam messages have spelling mistakes or odd wording.

What should you do? Do not click, do not reply and do not send anything. Instead, check by using a number or app that you already know is real, such as the number on the back of your bank card. And tell a trusted adult.

## 6.3 Passwords and codes

A **password** is a secret word or phrase that proves it is really you. A good password is long, hard to guess and used only once. A great trick is to join four random words together, such as "mango-lantern-river-seven". It is long, easy for you to remember and very hard for anyone else to guess. Do not use your name, your birthday, your phone number or "password123", because those are the first things a person would try.

Two more rules matter a lot. First, use a different password for each important account, so if one is stolen the others stay safe. Second, never share your passwords, PINs or OTPs with anyone, not even a friend.

An **OTP**, or one-time password, is a number sent to your phone that works only once. Banks and apps send it to check that you are really you. If someone asks you for your OTP, they are almost certainly trying to get into your account. Using a code like this on top of your password is called **two-step verification**. It is like having a second lock on your door.

## 6.4 Safe habits

- **Keep your phone and apps updated.** Updates often fix security holes.
- **Install apps only from official stores,** and read what permissions they ask for.
- **Be careful on public Wi-Fi.** Do not do banking on a network you do not trust.
- **Log out on shared computers,** such as at a cyber café or a school computer room.
- **Do not overshare.** Photos that show your school, your house or your location tell strangers more than you think.
- **Back up what matters,** such as your photos and contacts.
- **Ask for help.** If something feels wrong, stop and tell someone you trust. Nobody gets in trouble for asking.

## 6.5 Words to know

- **cybersecurity**: protecting people, devices and information online.
- **phishing**: a fake message that tries to trick you into giving away secrets or clicking a bad link.
- **scam**: a trick to take your money or information.
- **password**: a secret word or phrase that proves it is you.
- **PIN**: a secret number, for example for your ATM card.
- **OTP (one-time password)**: a code that works only once, sent to your phone.
- **two-step verification**: using a password and a code together.
- **malware**: harmful software that is made to damage your device or steal from you.
- **update**: a new version of software that often fixes problems.

## 6.6 Try it

Be a scam detective. Read these three messages and, for each one, write down whether you would trust it and why.

- Message one: "URGENT: Your account will be blocked in one hour. Click the link and send your OTP."
- Message two: "Hello Chidi, quiz practice is moved to 4 pm tomorrow. Please tell your team. Mrs Okeke." (from a number saved in your phone)
- Message three: "CONGRATULATIONS! You have won ₦500,000. Send ₦5,000 to claim your prize today."

Which warning signs from Section 6.2 can you find? Make up a scam message of your own and swap with a friend to spot the signs.

**Example 1**

A) Not click or reply, and check with the bank using its official number or app, because real banks never ask for OTPs
B) Reply quickly with the OTP, because waiting could get the account blocked and then she would not be able to use it
C) Click the link just to look at the page, and decide whether to share the OTP only after she has read it

*Walk through it:* The message has two warning signs: it hurries her ("today") and it asks for an OTP, which a real bank never does. Choice B does exactly what the scammer wants. # Chapter 7: Programming and Apps

## In this chapter

Chioma has never cooked jollof rice before. Her mother does not just say "make jollof rice". She gives clear steps: wash the rice, fry the tomato mix, add the water, cover the pot, wait. Chioma follows the steps in order and the rice comes out well. A computer needs steps like these too, and writing them is called programming.

By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

- Explain what a program and an algorithm are.
- Describe three building blocks of a program: steps in order, repeating, and making decisions.
- Explain what a variable is, and what a bug is.
- Tell an app from a website, and say what makes both of them work.

## 7.1 Instructions for a machine

A **program** is a list of instructions written for a computer. An **algorithm** is the plan of steps used to solve a problem, just like a recipe. Writing the program is called **coding** or **programming**.

Computers cannot guess. Every step must be clear, in the right order and complete. If Chioma forgets the step "add water", the rice stays hard. If she does the steps in the wrong order, for example serving the rice before cooking it, the result is nonsense. The same is true for code: a missing or misplaced step makes the program fail.

## 7.2 Three building blocks

Nearly every program is built from three simple ideas.

- **Sequence.** Steps are done one after another, in order. First wash, then fry, then add water.
- **Loop.** A loop repeats a step. Instead of writing "stir, stir, stir..." ten times, we write "repeat 10 times: stir".
- **Decision.** A program can choose what to do using "if" and "else". For example: IF the power is off, THEN start the generator, ELSE keep it off.

With just these three ideas you can describe a surprising number of things, from a game to a banking app.

## 7.3 Variables: boxes with names

A **variable** is a named place in the computer's memory that holds a value, and the value can change. Think of a bucket with a label on it. A bucket labelled "rice" might hold three cups today and five cups tomorrow.

In a football game, the computer keeps a variable called "score". It starts at 0, and each time a goal is scored, the program adds 1. The name stays the same, but the number changes.

## 7.4 Languages and bugs

Deep down, computers only understand 0s and 1s. People would find it very hard to write like that, so we use **programming languages**, such as Python and JavaScript. They look more like normal writing, and a tool translates them for the computer.

Sometimes a program does not do what we wanted. The mistake is called a **bug**. Perhaps a step is in the wrong order, or someone typed a plus instead of a minus. Finding and fixing bugs is called **debugging**. Every programmer, even the experts, makes bugs all the time. It is a normal part of the job, not a sign that you are bad at it.

## 7.5 Apps and websites

An **app** is a program that you install on your phone or computer, such as WhatsApp, a banking app or a game. A **website** is a set of pages that you open in a browser, and the pages are kept on a server (Chapter 1).

How websites are built, and what HTML, CSS and JavaScript do, is the topic of the next chapter. Many apps and websites also talk to a server to save things, such as your messages or your bank balance.

## 7.6 Words to know

- **program**: a list of instructions for a computer.
- **algorithm**: a plan of steps for solving a problem, like a recipe.
- **coding** or **programming**: writing instructions for a computer.
- **sequence**: doing steps one after another, in order.
- **loop**: repeating a step many times.
- **decision (if and else)**: choosing what to do depending on a condition.
- **variable**: a named place that holds a value that can change.
- **programming language**: a way of writing instructions that a computer can be made to follow, such as Python.
- **bug**: a mistake in a program.
- **debugging**: finding and fixing bugs.
- **app**: a program that you install on a device.
- **website**: a set of pages you open in a browser.

## 7.7 Try it

Write an algorithm for something you do every day, such as getting ready for school or sending a voice note. Number your steps. Add at least one loop (for example, "repeat until the bucket is full") and one decision (for example, "IF it is raining, THEN take an umbrella"). Now swap with a friend. Your friend must follow your steps exactly, even if they seem silly. Where did the instructions go wrong? Those are your bugs. Fix them together.

## 7.8 Check yourself

**Example 1**

*Segun writes these steps for making tea: 1. Pour the tea into the cup. 2. Boil the water. 3. Put the tea bag in the cup. His friend follows them exactly and makes a mess. What went wrong?*

A) The instructions had too few words, and a longer paragraph of explanation would have fixed the problem
B) The friend made an error, because well-written instructions can never be the cause of a mistake
C) The instructions needed a loop, because every task has to repeat several times before it works properly
D) The steps were in the wrong order, and a computer program also needs its steps in the right order to work

*Walk through it:* Pouring the tea before boiling the water and before putting in the tea bag gives a mess, even though the friend followed the steps perfectly. Choice A and choice C add things the task does not need, such as more words or a loop. Choice B blames the friend, but the friend did exactly what was written. The problem is the order of the steps, which is a bug, and programs have bugs like this too. So the answer is **D**.

*A football game keeps a number for the goals scored. It starts at 0, and each time a goal is scored, the number goes up by 1. In programming, what is this stored number called?*

A) A bug, because it is a number that changes by itself and so it may cause errors in the game

# Chapter 8: Making Websites

## In this chapter

Tunde plays for his school football team in Lagos. The team wants a web page that shows the fixtures and results, so that parents can check without calling the coach. Tunde's older cousin says, "I can show you how a web page is made. It is easier than you think." In this chapter you will see what happens when you open a website, and how a simple page is built.

By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

- Explain, step by step, what happens when you open a website.
- Say what HTML, CSS and JavaScript each do on a web page.
- Read a few simple HTML tags and say what they mean.
- Explain why web pages should work well on phones and should not use too much data.
- Say what it means to put a website online.

## 8.1 What happens when you open a website?

Every website has an address, called a **URL**. The main part of it, such as `myschoolteam.ng`, is the **domain name**. A domain name is easy for people to remember. Computers find each other using number addresses, and the internet has a kind of phone book that turns a domain name into the right number. The ending `.ng` is the one that belongs to Nigeria.

Here is what happens when you open a page:

1. You type the address into a **browser**, the app you use to open websites.
2. The browser finds the server (Chapter 1) that holds the website.
3. The browser asks the server for the page.
4. The server sends the files for the page back to your phone.
5. The browser reads the files and shows the page on your screen.

All of this takes only a moment. The part you can see, the page on your screen, is called the **front end**. The part that works behind the scenes on the server, such as saving a result or checking a password, is called the **back end**.

## 8.2 Three building blocks

Every web page is built from three tools that work together. Think of a house.

- **HTML** builds the structure: the headings, paragraphs, pictures and links. It is like the walls and rooms of the house.
- **CSS** controls the look: the colours, the fonts, the sizes and the spacing. It is like the paint and the furniture.
- **JavaScript** makes things happen: menus that open, buttons that react, games that run. It is like the light switches and the doors.

All three are written as plain text. The browser reads that text and builds the page from it.

## 8.3 A first look at HTML

HTML uses **tags** to say what each piece of text is. Most tags come in pairs: one tag opens, and a second tag with a slash closes. Here is the start of Tunde's team page:

- `<h1>Eagles Junior FC</h1>` is a big heading.
- `<p>Next match: Saturday at 3 pm.</p>` is a paragraph.
- `<a href="results.html">See our results</a>` is a link that takes you to another page.
- `<img src="team.jpg" alt="Our team">` is a picture. The alt text describes the picture for people who cannot see it, and it also shows if the picture does not load.

Notice that the tags do not say anything about colour or size. They only say what each piece is. Looks are the job of CSS.

## 8.4 A little CSS and JavaScript

CSS is written as short rules. The first part says what to change, and the part inside the curly brackets says how:

- `p { color: green; }` makes every paragraph green.
- `h1 { font-size: 40px; }` makes every big heading larger.

JavaScript is for actions. You can tell the page, "When someone taps this button, show the match time." That is a decision, like the if and else you met in Chapter 7. JavaScript uses the same building blocks as every other programming language: steps in order, loops and decisions.

## 8.5 Pages for phones, and pages for data

Many people in Nigeria go online mainly with a phone. So a good web page must work well on a small screen. This is called **responsive** design. The layout changes to fit the screen, the text can be read without zooming, and the buttons are big enough to tap with a thumb.

Data also costs money. Big pictures and videos use a lot of a data bundle, and they load slowly on a weak network. A good developer makes pictures smaller and keeps pages light, so the page opens quickly even when the network is poor. A light page is a kind page, because it respects the person's data.

## 8.6 Putting a website online

The files for your page, kept on your own computer, can only be seen by you. To share the page with the world, the files must be put on a server that is always switched on and connected. This is called **hosting**. Companies rent out space on their servers, and some offer free plans for small sites. You also choose a domain name, such as one ending in `.ng` or `.com`.

Once the files are uploaded and the domain name points to them, anyone can type the address and see your page. Doing this is called **publishing**. Before you publish, check the spelling, test the page on a phone, and make sure you are not sharing private information, as you learned in Chapter 6.

## 8.7 Who builds websites?

A **front-end developer** builds the part that people see and touch. A **back-end developer** builds the part that works on the server. A **designer** plans how the page looks and feels. In a small team, one person often does several of these jobs. People build websites for all sorts of things: a school club, a tailor, a caterer, a church group or a shop. You can start learning with free tools and a simple text editor.

## 8.8 Words to know

- **web page**: one page of a website, shown in a browser.
- **browser**: the app you use to open websites.
- **URL**: the address of a web page.
- **domain name**: the easy-to-remember name in a web address, such as `myschoolteam.ng`.
- **front end**: the part of a website that you see and use.
- **back end**: the part that works behind the scenes on the server.
- **HTML**: the tool that builds the structure of a web page.
- **tag**: a label in HTML that says what a piece of text is, such as a heading or a paragraph.
- **CSS**: the tool that controls how a web page looks.
- **JavaScript**: the tool that makes a web page react and do things.
- **responsive**: a page that fits well on any size of screen.
- **hosting**: keeping a website's files on a server that is always connected.
- **publishing**: putting a website online so that others can see it.

Plan a web page for something you care about, such as your football team, a family business or a school club. On paper, sketch the page. Where is the heading? Where are the picture, the text and the button? Label each part with the HTML idea it needs: heading, paragraph, image or link. Then write one wish for CSS ("make the heading green") and one wish for JavaScript ("when I tap the button, show the match time").

Change the words, save, and refresh the page to see your change appear.

# Chapter 9: Data

## In this chapter

Mama Ngozi runs a small provision shop in Onitsha. At the end of every week she is short of bread and sachet water, but she has too much of something else. She starts writing down what she sells each day. After a month she can see exactly which things sell fastest, and she stops buying too much of the wrong things. Without knowing it, she has started using data.

By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

- Explain what data is, with examples from daily life.
- Collect simple data and organise it so it can be read.
- Find the average and the most common value, and explain why charts help.
- Explain why a small or one-sided group of answers can mislead.
- Say what a data scientist does.

## 9.1 What is data?

**Data** means facts that we collect: numbers, words, pictures or sounds. A class register is data. So are the scores in a football league, the temperature each day, and the number of sachet water Mama Ngozi sells on Monday.

On their own, data are just facts. When we organise them and study them, they turn into **information** that helps us make good decisions. Mama Ngozi's notes are only data. Knowing "bread sells fastest on Fridays, so I should buy more on Thursday" is information.

Your phone collects data about you too: your steps, your battery use and the apps you open. This is one reason we must think about privacy, which you will meet in Chapter 10.

## 9.2 Collecting and organising

The first step is to collect data in a careful way. A simple method is a **survey**: asking people the same question and writing down their answers. A good way to record answers is a **table**, with rows and columns.

For example, Mama Ngozi could keep a table for her week:

- Monday: 40 sachets of water, 12 loaves of bread
- Tuesday: 35 sachets of water, 18 loaves of bread
- Wednesday: 50 sachets of water, 15 loaves of bread
- Thursday: 45 sachets of water, 15 loaves of bread
- Friday: 30 sachets of water, 20 loaves of bread

Once the data is in a table, it is much easier to see patterns than when the numbers are in your head.

## 9.3 Making sense of the numbers

There are a few simple tools for studying data:

- **Total.** Add everything up. For the sachet water, 40 + 35 + 50 + 45 + 30 = 200.
- **Average (mean).** Add all the values and divide by how many there are. The average sachet water per day is 200 ÷ 5 = 40.
- **Most common.** The value that appears most often. For the bread, the number 15 appears twice, so it is the most common.
- **Charts.** A **bar chart** turns numbers into bars, so you can see the biggest and smallest at a glance. People find a picture much faster to read than a list.

A word of warning about charts: always look at the numbers along the side. A chart can make a small difference look huge if those numbers start at a big value instead of zero.

## 9.4 Good data and bad data

Data is only useful if it is fair and correct. Suppose Chidera wants to know which football club is the favourite in her school, but she only asks the boys in the school team. Her answer will not show what the whole school thinks. A group of people you ask is called a **sample**, and a sample needs to be a fair mix of the people you want to know about. Asking more people from different classes, ages and backgrounds gives a better picture.

Data can also be wrong because of typing mistakes, missing answers or old information. Good people who work with data check it first.

Machine learning (Chapter 3) learns from data, so poor or unfair data leads to poor or unfair results. Someone who collects, cleans and studies data to find useful patterns is called a **data scientist**. Many businesses, hospitals, farms and schools now need people with this skill.

## 9.5 Words to know

- **data**: facts we collect, such as numbers, words, pictures or sounds.
- **information**: data that has been organised and studied so it is useful.
- **survey**: asking many people the same question and recording the answers.
- **table**: a way of organising data in rows and columns.
- **total**: all the values added together.
- **average (mean)**: the total divided by how many values there are.
- **bar chart**: a chart that shows numbers as bars.
- **sample**: the group of people or things you collect data from.
- **data scientist**: a person who collects and studies data to find useful patterns.

## 9.6 Try it

Do a mini survey. Ask ten people a simple question, such as "What is your favourite Nigerian dish?" Write down each answer as a tally mark on paper. Count the totals and find the most common answer. Draw a bar chart with one bar for each dish. Then ask yourself: did I ask a fair mix of people, or only people like me? How could I make my sample better?

**Example 1**

A) It may not be fair for the whole school, because ten friends from one class are not a good mix of all the students
B) It is exactly correct for the whole school, because students in the same school usually like the same subjects
C) It is useless, because surveys must ask every single one of the 600 students or they cannot say anything at all

*Walk through it:* Ten friends from one class are a small and one-sided sample. Choice B assumes everyone in a school likes the same things, which is not true. Choice C goes too far: you do not need to ask all 600, but you need a fair mix. Choice D confuses being honest with being fair, because friends can be honest and still not represent the whole school. So the answer is **A**.

**Example 2**

*Mama Ngozi sold 12, 18, 15, 15 and 20 loaves of bread on five days. What was her average number of loaves per day?*

A) 15, because it is the number that appears most often in the list of numbers
B) 16, because the total of 80 loaves divided by 5 days gives 16
C) 20, because it is the largest number, and the average is the biggest value
D) 80, because the average is what you get when you add up all the days together

*Walk through it:* The total is 12 + 18 + 15 + 15 + 20 = 80, and 80 divided by 5 days is 16. Choice A gives the most common number, which is a different idea. Choice C gives the largest number, which is not the average either. Choice D stops at the total and forgets to divide. So the answer is **B**.

# Chapter 10: Technology and Everyone

## In this chapter

Emeka's school tries a new attendance app that takes attendance by looking at students' faces. It works for most students, but a few are marked absent every day even though they are sitting in class. Is that fair? Who should fix it? Technology is powerful, and with power comes responsibility. In this last chapter you will think about how to make technology fair, safe and useful for everyone.

By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

- Explain why technology can be unfair, and who is responsible for fixing it.
- Explain why your personal information matters, and how to protect it.
- Explain what the digital divide is.
- Describe how technology changes jobs, and imagine a good use of technology in your own community.

## 10.1 Is technology fair?

Technology is built by people, using data collected by people. So it can copy people's mistakes. Remember the mango story in Chapter 3: a machine only knows what its examples taught it. Some face-recognition systems have made more mistakes for some groups of people than for others, because the examples they learned from did not include a fair mix of faces.

When technology is unfair, it can cause real harm, like Emeka's classmates being marked absent. Who should fix it? The people who make the technology should test it with many different people before it is used. The people who choose it, such as a school, should check that it works for everyone, and have a simple way to correct mistakes. And everyone can help by speaking up when something is not working fairly.

## 10.2 Your information matters

**Personal information** is anything that says who you are: your full name, address, phone number, school, photos, passwords, bank details, BVN and your location.

Many free apps earn money by showing adverts based on what you do. This is why people say, "If an app is free, you may be paying with your information." That is not always bad, but you should know it is happening.

Some good habits:

- **Ask why.** If an app asks for your contacts, camera, location or microphone, ask yourself whether it really needs them to do its job.
- **Allow only what is needed.** You can say no to a permission, and often the app still works.
- **Think before you post.** Do not share your address, your school uniform or your ID card. Once something is online, you cannot fully take it back.
- **Respect other people's privacy.** Ask before you post someone else's photo.

Nigeria has laws that protect people's personal data, and organisations are expected to follow them.

## 10.3 Technology for everyone?

Not everyone has the same chances to use technology. Some people have no smartphone, or cannot afford data. Some places have a weak network or no steady electricity. Some people have never been shown how to use a computer. The gap between people who can easily use technology and people who cannot is called the **digital divide**.

The gap matters because more and more things, such as school work, banking and finding jobs, depend on being online. People are working on this in many ways, such as apps that work without a lot of data, shared computer rooms in communities, and solar power for charging. You could be one of the people who helps close the gap.

## 10.4 Technology and work

New technology changes jobs. Some tasks that people used to do by hand are now done by machines. But new jobs also appear. When mobile phones became common in Nigeria, many small businesses started, such as phone repair, selling airtime and data, and POS agents. Today there are new jobs in building apps, working with data, flying drones for farms and teaching others online.

What stays valuable, whatever the technology? Curiosity, honesty, working well in a team, solving problems, and the habit of learning new things. That is why learning how to learn is the best skill you can build now.

## 10.5 Using technology well

Technology is a tool, and you decide how to use it. Be kind online, because words hurt just as much on a screen as in person, and bullying online is still bullying. Think before you post or forward something. Take breaks from screens, sleep well and spend time with people face to face. Use technology to create and learn, not only to scroll. And if you use an AI tool for schoolwork, be honest about it, and use it to understand things better, not to skip learning.

## 10.6 Words to know

- **fairness**: treating people equally and giving everyone a fair chance.
- **bias**: when technology works worse for some people because of the examples it learned from.
- **privacy**: your right to keep your personal information to yourself.
- **personal information**: details that say who you are, such as your name, address and photos.
- **permission**: something an app asks to use on your device, such as your camera or location.
- **digital divide**: the gap between people who can easily use technology and people who cannot.
- **cyberbullying**: being unkind or threatening to someone online.
- **responsible**: thinking about the effect of what you do and taking care of it.

## 10.8 Check yourself

**Example 1**

*Emeka's school attendance app works for most students, but it often marks a few students absent when they are present, because it does not recognise their faces. What is the best next step?*

A) Ignore the problem, because a system that works for most students is always good enough for a school
B) Blame the students, because a computer cannot make mistakes and so the students must be doing something wrong
C) Test the app with many different faces and fix the problem, and let a teacher mark attendance until it is fixed
D) Remove the app's data and let it learn only from the students that it already recognises well

*Walk through it:* Choice A accepts that some students will be treated unfairly. Choice B is wrong because computers do make mistakes, especially when they learned from limited examples. Choice D would make the problem worse, because learning only from the faces it already recognises means it never learns the others. The fair answer is to test with a wider mix of faces and to have a back-up so no student is marked absent by mistake. So the answer is **C**.

**Example 2**

*A free game asks for access to your contacts, camera, location and microphone. What is the most sensible thing to do?*

A) Ask why a game needs all of that, allow only what it really needs, and ask a parent or teacher if you are unsure
B) Allow everything, because free apps are always safe and only the ones you pay for can ever be dangerous
C) Allow everything at once, because the game will not work at all if even one permission is refused
D) Refuse and delete every app on your phone, because all apps collect your information in a harmful way

*Walk through it:* Choice B and choice C both say to allow everything, but a game rarely needs your contacts or microphone, and many apps still work when you refuse a permission. Choice D goes too far, because many apps are useful and safe. The sensible step is to ask why each permission is needed, allow only what is necessary and get advice if you are unsure. So the answer is **A**.
